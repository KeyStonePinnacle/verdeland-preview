What Does New Construction Mean? A Nassau County Buyer’s Guide

Published October 21, 2026

New construction generally means a newly built home rather than an existing home being resold. In a listing, however, that phrase alone does not tell you whether the house is finished, which selections are included, or what remains before you can move in.

For buyers in Nassau County, Long Island, a useful starting point is to ask three questions: What exactly is being built? Which local authority approves it? What does the written purchase agreement promise?

New construction, renovated homes and custom builds

These descriptions can refer to different buying experiences:

  • A completed new home can be toured in its finished condition. Ask whether required occupancy approvals are issued and which, if any, items remain unfinished.
  • A home under construction may have a planned layout and finish package, with some decisions still open. Confirm the selection deadlines and available choices in writing.
  • A preconstruction purchase involves a home that has not yet been completed, and sometimes has not started. Drawings and renderings help explain the plan, but the signed specifications should establish what the buyer receives.
  • A custom build usually involves more buyer input. Establish who owns the land, who is responsible for approvals and how changes affect price and timing.
  • A renovated home is an existing building that has been updated. New kitchens, windows or finishes do not by themselves establish that the entire house is newly constructed.

Ask specifically whether any existing foundation, framing or other building components are retained. Avoid relying on marketing terminology to resolve construction scope or legal warranty coverage.

Why the exact Nassau County address matters

Start with the property's municipality, not just its mailing address. Nassau County directs building and zoning inquiries to the applicable city, town or village, while some projects also involve county approvals. Its building and zoning directory is a useful starting point.

New York's Department of State advises contacting the local building department for project-specific code and compliance requirements. State building-code guidance also distinguishes New York City's building-code system from the rest of the state.

For example, the Town of Hempstead Building Department is a resource for properties within its jurisdiction. Do not assume the town handles every property with a nearby postal address, particularly when an incorporated village is involved.

Read the specifications alongside the floor plan

A photograph shows an appearance. A specification identifies an obligation.

Before signing, ask for a written list covering:

  1. The floor plan, room dimensions and how advertised square footage is calculated.
  2. Kitchen cabinets, countertops, appliances and plumbing fixtures.
  3. Flooring, doors, windows, trim and paint allowances.
  4. Heating, cooling and ventilation equipment and controls.
  5. Driveway, walkways, grading, landscaping and fencing included in the price.
  6. Basement and attic condition, permitted use and any unfinished areas.
  7. Included selections versus upgrades, allowances and possible substitutions.

Ask how a requested change is documented, priced and approved. Keep renderings, selections and change orders with the final contract documents so everyone works from the same record.

Understand the schedule before planning your move

Ask the seller to explain the current construction stage and the remaining milestones. A target completion date should be distinguished from a contractual closing obligation.

Useful questions include:

  • Which permits and inspections are complete, and which remain?
  • What events could change the expected closing date?
  • When must selections be finalized?
  • How will progress and schedule changes be communicated?
  • What happens if your mortgage rate lock or current housing arrangements expire first?

Review these issues with your attorney and lender before making commitments that depend on one exact move-in date. No general article can predict the completion date of an individual home.

Keep the inspection, walkthrough and occupancy approval separate

These steps serve different purposes. Arrange an independent home inspection appropriate to the construction stage, subject to the agreed access terms. Use the final walkthrough to compare the house with the contract, test accessible features and document remaining work.

Separately, confirm the required municipal occupancy documentation. In the Town of Hempstead, the published code generally prohibits occupancy of a newly erected building before a certificate of occupancy is issued. Review the town's occupancy provisions and confirm the requirements for the particular address with the responsible authority.

Prepare a written punch list with locations, photographs and an agreed process for addressing each item. Ask your attorney how unfinished work will be handled at closing rather than assuming every walkthrough item automatically delays it.

Request a closing and ownership file

Before closing, request the documents relevant to your transaction:

  • Executed contract, specifications and approved changes.
  • Required occupancy approvals and applicable inspection records.
  • Survey and title materials reviewed by your attorney.
  • Written builder warranty, claim instructions and named contact.
  • Manufacturer documents, equipment model numbers and maintenance instructions.
  • A written record of outstanding items and agreed next steps.

A newly built home still needs maintenance. Learn the shutoff locations and operating procedures before move-in, and keep service records afterward.

Frequently asked questions

Does new construction mean the home is move-in ready?

Not necessarily. Ask whether construction, agreed finishing work and required occupancy approvals are complete, and confirm the closing arrangements.

Should I inspect a brand-new home?

An independent inspection is a useful way to assess its condition and identify questions before closing. Agree on access and timing with the seller; it does not replace municipal approvals or the contract review.

Are all finishes in a model or rendering included?

Do not assume so. Ask for the included specifications and a separate list of optional upgrades or illustrative features.

Where should I start when comparing new homes?

Compare the written scope, location, layout, current construction stage, purchase terms and ongoing ownership needs. An attractive finish package is only one part of the decision.
